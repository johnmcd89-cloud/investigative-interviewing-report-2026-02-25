**Bottom line**
- Credibility assessment is most reliable when it is **structured, corroboration-led, and uncertainty-explicit**.
- Single-cue lie detection is weak: average unaided lie/truth judgment is about **54%** (near chance), and nonverbal “tells” are generally weak/unreliable.
- The most defensible gains come from **how you interview** (strategic evidence handling, detail elicitation, corroboration), not from reading demeanor.

**What does and does not predict deception**
- `Weak / often misleading`: gaze aversion, fidgeting, nervousness, “calm = truthful,” or other demeanor heuristics.
- `Better-supported`: statement–evidence inconsistencies created via strategic questioning, lower proportion of verifiable details, and content patterns under well-designed interviewing.
- `Important caveat`: consistency alone is not truth. Truthful people also vary across recalls, especially on peripheral details and after trauma.

**Myths vs validated indicators**
- `Myth`: “Body language reveals lies reliably.”
- `Evidence`: nonverbal cues are faint/unreliable; observers focusing on behavior perform poorly.
- `Validated direction (context-dependent)`: verbal/content cues plus independent evidence checks outperform demeanor-based judgment.

**Statement analysis approaches**
- `CBCA / SVA`: some discriminative validity (especially in constrained uses), but not a stand-alone truth machine.
- `Reality Monitoring`: useful in some contexts, moderate effects, needs trained application.
- `SCAN`: lacks empirical support; do not use as sole or primary credibility method.

**Corroboration + consistency testing**
- Prioritize **material facts** and test against independent records/witnesses/forensics.
- Separate:
- Internal consistency (within account).
- External consistency (with independent evidence/country info/other records).
- Plausibility (contextual, not “would this happen in my culture?”).
- Treat peripheral inconsistencies cautiously; assess explanations and vulnerability factors before adverse findings.

**SUE, cognitive load, timeline/detail checks**
- `Strategic Use of Evidence (SUE)`: withhold key evidence early, elicit full account first, disclose strategically later to test statement–evidence fit.
- `Cognitive-load tools`: reverse-order recall, unexpected questions, and detail-expansion prompts can increase diagnostic value.
- `Timeline/detail checks`: event timeline anchoring, spatial sketching, and verifiable-detail probing improve testability.
- `Safeguard`: cognitive-load tactics can burden truthful but vulnerable interviewees too; use proportionately.

**How institutions assess credibility**
- `Courts`: commonly weigh memory quality, bias/motive, consistency with other evidence, and reasonableness; model instructions warn not to treat inconsistency alone as falsity.
- `US immigration/asylum law`: statute explicitly uses totality factors (demeanor, plausibility, internal/external consistency, inaccuracies, corroboration).
- `UK asylum practice guidance`: structured, holistic credibility assessment; explicit warning that general demeanor should not determine credibility; requires considering trauma, memory, culture, language, interpreter effects.
- `EU asylum adjudication`: structured 3-step evidence/credibility/risk approach; judicial guidance for tribunals.
- `Intelligence/integrity functions`: source-quality and information-credibility grading, explicit uncertainty/confidence expression, auditability.

**Practical defensible protocol (bias-reducing)**
1. Define material issues and rival hypotheses before interview.
2. Map available evidence and planned corroboration points.
3. Use information-gathering interview structure (PEACE-type flow).
4. Obtain uninterrupted free narrative first.
5. Elicit testable detail (who/when/where/how verified), then timeline/sketch.
6. Ask unexpected but fair questions on core and peripheral elements.
7. Apply SUE (late/strategic disclosure) to test statement–evidence consistency.
8. Evaluate internal consistency, external consistency, plausibility, and verifiable detail ratio separately.
9. Record motive-to-fabricate and motive-to-conceal, but never as sole proof.
10. Explicitly account for trauma/PTSD, memory science, age, mental health, culture/language, and interpretation effects.
11. De-weight demeanor unless strongly corroborated by objective evidence.
12. Require documented reasoning, uncertainty/confidence statement, and second-review for adverse credibility findings.

Inference: where legal frameworks include demeanor, evidence quality improves if agencies/judges **de-emphasize demeanor** and **weight corroborated content + methodologically sound interviewing** more heavily.
